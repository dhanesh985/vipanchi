Tala Lab

Pick a tala, press Play, and keep it with your hand. A clap opens every anga. In a laghu you then count on your fingers starting from the little finger. A drutam is a clap and a wave of the palm. The talam cymbal rings on samam, the first beat, where phrases land.

Gati (pulses inside each beat)

All 35 Suladi talas

Seven anga patterns across five jatis. The jati sets how many beats a laghu (I) has. The gold dot marks the jati used in the sapta tala alankarams that students learn first.

Gati, also called nadai

The tala fixes where the beats fall. Gati decides how each beat is divided. Any of the 35 talas can run in any of the five gatis, which is where the figure of 175 talas comes from.

Chapu talas

Compact talas kept by marking each group with a beat instead of finger counts. They come from folk and dance practice and are common in kritis, padams and devotional songs.

Sthayis and the 72 system

The registers a raga is sung in, and the rules that generate the 72 parent scales.

Sthayi: the registers

A sthayi is one octave, from Sa up to the next Sa. The same swara can be sung in a lower, middle or upper sthayi, and notation marks which one with dots.

Madhya sthayi is home. An alapana usually settles there, dips into mandra sthayi, then climbs to tara sthayi for its peak before returning. Most voices use about two and a half sthayis.

12 places, 16 names

Within one sthayi there are 12 note positions. Sa and Pa never change. Ri, Ga, Ma, Dha and Ni have variants, and four positions carry two names, which is why there are 16 swara names for 12 places.

6 × 6 × 2 = 72

A melakarta uses all seven swaras, one variant each, going straight up and straight down. Ri and Ga can pair in six ways. Dha and Ni can pair in the same six ways. Ma is either Shuddha or Prati.

Melas 1–36 use Shuddha Ma, 37–72 use Prati Ma. Each block of six is a chakra. Inside a chakra, Dha–Ni steps through the six pairs in order.

The number is in the name

Each melakarta name hides its number through the Katapayadi code. Every consonant stands for a digit. Read the first two syllables, then reverse the digits.

Venkatamakhin laid out the 72-scale scheme in the 17th century. The names used today, with this built-in numbering, come from Govindacharya's later list. Muthuswami Dikshitar followed an older naming, the asampurna melas, and those raga names appear on this map as janyas.

Arohana and avarohana are the standard textbook outline. Real ragas live in their phrases and gamakas, so use the Listen links to hear how each one actually moves. The scale player uses equal-tempered pitches without gamakas. It is a guide to the notes, not to the raga's sound. Composition lists draw on the published 72-melakarta cycle of Koteeswara Iyer and the catalogue of Muthuswami Dikshitar's kritis. Spellings of titles vary between sources.
